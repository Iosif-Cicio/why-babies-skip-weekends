How histograms work

Why do fewer babies arrive on weekends?

We counted US births for every single day from 1994 to 2014: 7,670 days and over 85 million babies, about 4 million per year. A list of 7,670 numbers tells you nothing at a glance. A histogram does.

Sorting days into bins

Each of the 7,670 days had a certain number of births. A histogram sorts those days into ranges called bins, here we sort it into 5,000 to 10,000 births, 10,000 to 15,000, and above 15,000.

Each bar shows how many days ended up in that bin. The taller the bar, the more days had that many births. With these three wide bins, we learn that most days had 10,000 to 15,000 births. Only 17 of the 7,670 days had more than 15,000 births, so the third bar is barely visible.

Common mistake: too many bins

With about 200 narrow bins, the chart shows more detail, but it also gets harder to read. Each bin holds only a few days now, so the bars jump up and down a lot more, creating noise. Some of the extra detail is valid, though. Notice how the left peak splits into two humps? We'll find out why a little further down, but through the lens of a better design.

Fewer bins, clearer picture

With about 30 bins, the noise is smoothed out, less distractions, and the big picture is easier to read: two peaks, one around 8,000 births and one around 12,300. This shape is called bimodal, one of the typical shapes to look for in a histogram. It is usually a hint that two different groups are mixed in the data.

When the average misleads

Imagine a room with five kids aged 5 and two grandparents aged 75. Their average age is 25, but nobody in the room is 25.

Our data has the same problem. The mean is 11,132 births per day (85.4 million births ÷ 7,670 days). It lands between the two peaks, and most days are clearly above or clearly below it.

The median, the middle day when you sort them all, is 11,941. It sits closer to the big peak, but it doesn't describe the small one either.

So who are these two groups? The next step shows it.

Weekdays vs. weekends

Colour each day by weekday or weekend and the two peaks separate. On an average weekday about 12,300 babies are born, on a weekend day only about 8,100. Many births are planned, like C-sections and inductions, and hospitals schedule them on weekdays.

Just like the kids and grandparents, the mean mixed two groups: five weekdays and two weekend days per week. Now that we know what to look for, we can use narrower bins again, about 80, to see more detail. The two humps in the left peak are back: those are Saturdays and Sundays, about 8,600 births on an average Saturday, only about 7,600 on a Sunday.

The odd ones out

A few blue weekdays sit in the low peak: 152 of all 5,478 weekdays. These are outliers, values that don't fit their group. Every single one of them is a holiday or a day right next to one: Christmas, New Year, July 4th, Thanksgiving, Memorial Day and Labor Day. Hospitals avoid scheduling births on those days, just like on weekends.

What we learned about histograms

The bin size decides what a histogram can show: too few bins hide the shape, more bins add detail but also noise. The shape itself tells a story. Two peaks hint at two hidden groups, and in that case the mean alone can be misleading. Always look at the distribution, not just the average.
