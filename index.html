How histograms work

Why Babies Skip Weekends

Do babies prefer weekdays?

Between 1994 and 2014, the US recorded births for every single day: 7,670 days and over 85 million babies, about 4 million per year. But when we take a closer look at these numbers, something unexpected appears: fewer babies seem to be born on weekends.

Is this just a coincidence, or is there something more behind it? Let's explore the data, one histogram at a time, and find out what the numbers can tell us.

First, let's make sense of the data!

We have birth records from 7,670 days. To make sense of all these numbers, we use a histogram. It groups days with similar birth counts into ranges called bins. For example, all days with 5,000 to 10,000 births belong to one bin.

The bottom axis shows births per day, while the left axis shows how many days fall into each bin. The taller the bar, the more days belong to that group.

You can see two large bars, but there are actually three! The third bin contains only 17 days with 15,000 or more births, making its bar barely visible compared to the other two.

Most days had between 10,000 and 15,000 births. But what happens if we make the bins smaller?

More bins, more details. But is that better?

Let's make the bins much smaller! Instead of three wide groups, we now have around 200 narrow bins.

Each bar represents a smaller range of daily births. This reveals more detail, but also makes the histogram look a bit messy. The bars jump up and down because fewer days fall into each bin. Still, something interesting appears: two distinct peaks! One around 8,000 births per day and another around 12,000.

Why are there two groups of days with such different birth counts? Let's take a closer look!

Fewer bins, clearer picture!

Let's find a middle ground! Instead of 200 narrow bins, we now use around 30 wider bins.

With fewer bars, the chart looks less messy, making it easier to spot the overall pattern: two clear peaks, one around 8,000 births per day and another around 12,3s00. A distribution with two peaks is called bimodal. This can be a clue that two different groups are hidden within our data.

But would we have noticed these two peaks if we only looked at the average?

When the average misleads

Usually, we use averages to summarize large amounts of data. But can one number really describe a distribution with two peaks?

The mean (11,132 births) is calculated by dividing the total number of births by the number of days (85.4 million births ÷ 7,670 days). It's shown by the dashed line between the two peaks.

The median (11,941 births) is the middle value when we sort all days by their birth counts. It's shown by the dotted line, closer to the taller peak.

Although these numbers are relatively close, neither reveals the two peaks we discovered earlier. Mean and median help us summarize the data, but they can't tell us why there are two peaks. To understand that, we need to look at the days more closely.

The mystery revealed: Weekdays vs. weekends

We've discovered two distinct peaks in our histogram. But what makes some days so different from others?

To find out, let's look at the days behind the numbers. We've separated them into weekdays (Monday to Friday), shown in blue, and weekends (Saturday and Sunday), shown in orange. Now we can see something interesting: The smaller peak around 8,000 births per day is mostly made up of weekend days, while the larger peak around 12,300 births per day is mostly made up of weekdays.

We've also increased the number of bins to around 80, allowing us to see more detail. Unlike the earlier histograms, this one shows us which days are behind the two peaks.

So we've finally discovered the pattern: fewer babies are typically born on weekends than on weekdays.

The odd ones out

We've discovered that weekdays and weekends usually have different birth counts. But take a closer look: Why are there still some blue bars among the orange ones? These represent weekdays with unusually low birth counts. In fact, 152 out of 5,478 weekdays fall into this lower range. Such unusual values are often called outliers.

When we look at these days more closely, we find something interesting: They fall on or near major US holidays, such as Christmas, New Year's Day or Thanksgiving. Just like weekends, holidays tend to have fewer births. This suggests that the calendar may play a bigger role in birth patterns than we first thought.

The answer to: Why do babies skip weekends?

Babies don't follow calendars, but hospitals often do.

Not every birth happens the same way. Some babies arrive after spontaneous labour, which can begin at any time, day or night. Other births involve planned C-sections (surgical deliveries) or labour inductions (medically starting labour). When medically appropriate, these procedures can be scheduled in advance.

And that's where the day of the week comes into play. Research from the United States shows that labour inductions are more common on weekdays than on weekends [1]. The CDC also reports that the weekend drop in births is especially noticeable for C-sections, which are often scheduled during the working week [2]. This means that weekdays include both spontaneous births and many scheduled deliveries, while weekends generally have fewer of those planned procedures. This helps explain why the number of births tends to be higher from Monday to Friday. It may also help explain why certain holidays stand out in our data.

So, do babies really skip weekends? Not quite! Babies are still born every day. But medical scheduling can influence when some births happen. Our histograms revealed the pattern. Scientific research helps us understand why it occurs.

What we learned about histograms

The bin size decides what a histogram can show: too few bins hide the shape, more bins add detail but also noise. The shape itself tells a story. Two peaks hint at two hidden groups, and in that case the mean alone can be misleading. Bars that sit where they don't belong point to outliers worth a closer look.

By changing the bin size and comparing different groups, we turned thousands of daily birth counts into a pattern we could understand. The lesson? Always look at the distribution, not just the average.
